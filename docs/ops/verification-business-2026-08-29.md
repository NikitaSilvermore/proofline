# Becoming the proof layer — strategy

> **TIER: REFERENCE** — strategy and options, written 2026-08-29 from Nikita's
> brief: *"I want to become the proof layer for coaches in the info space...
> they pay a membership to me to get a console and weekly student intake form
> which will be verified so that they can legally share testimonials and data."*
> Builds on the already-drafted
> [`milestamp-verified-standard-DRAFT.md`](milestamp-verified-standard-DRAFT.md),
> which holds the five-rung ladder and the seven-point standard. **Nothing here
> is decided.**

---

## 1. The correction that changes the plan: you cannot become a regulator

A **regulator** is created by statute — the FCA, MHRA, CQC and Ofcom exist
because Parliament made them. You cannot apply to be one, and no amount of
diligence gets you there.

What you can be, starting immediately, is a **certification body operating a
certification mark**. That is what B Corp, Fairtrade, the Good Housekeeping
Institute, Which? Best Buy and every ISO certifier actually are: private
organisations whose authority is *earned through adoption*, not granted by law.

This is better news than it sounds. It is faster, needs no legislation, and the
good ones are more profitable than most regulators could dream of. But the
authority has to be built, and the first two years of a certification mark are
spent making it mean something.

**Two specifics worth knowing early, because they shape the entity:**

- A **certification mark** is a distinct kind of trademark in both the UK and
  the US, not an ordinary one. In the UK you must file **regulations governing
  use** with the IPO alongside the application. In the US the owner of a
  certification mark generally **may not use it on their own services** — which
  matters if Milestamp ever wanted to certify a programme it also ran.
- This sits on top of the trademark gate already in the roadmap (Milestone 9b).
  Raise certification marks with the attorney at the same time as the ordinary
  searches — it is one conversation, not two.

*(Both points to be confirmed with a trademark attorney; they are the right
questions to ask, not settled advice.)*

## 2. The moat is the baseline, not the badge

Everyone can verify an ending. A Stripe screenshot, a bank statement, a payout
dashboard — a dozen tools do this and it is nearly a commodity.

Almost nobody has a **verified, immutable, timestamped starting point, captured
before anyone knew how the story would end.** That is the rarest asset in the
whole of outcome marketing, because it cannot be reconstructed later. A "before"
that could not have been fabricated retroactively is worth more than any
"after".

Milestamp already captures exactly that, and has since the first build: the
intake locks, it cannot be edited, and the athlete's own words are stored with
it. **That is the product's actual defensibility.** Everything else — the
console, the cards, the score — is a delivery mechanism for it.

Strategic consequence: the pitch is not "we verify results". It is **"we are the
only people holding an unfakeable before."**

## 3. Verify the method, not the outcome — the notary model

The obvious way to build this is also the way that gets sued: certify that a
coach's clients earned £X. Then every disputed claim points at your deep pocket,
and your insurance premium becomes the business.

The safer and more sellable posture is a **notary**, not an **auditor**:

| Auditor says | Notary says |
|---|---|
| "These results are true." | "This baseline was recorded on 3 Feb and has not been altered. These 34 weekly reports were captured on these dates. This artefact was supplied on 12 Aug from this source." |
| Gives an opinion; carries opinion liability | Attests that a thing happened at a time; carries far narrower liability |

You certify **provenance, method and completeness** — never truth. Point 6 of
the drafted standard (scope disclosure: *"Verified: $12,000 in Stripe-processed
revenue during the programme. Not verified: profit, revenue outside Stripe"*)
already carries this instinct. Make it the whole philosophy rather than one
clause. It is simultaneously the honest position, the defensible position, and
— because it is unusually candid — the marketable one.

## 4. The conflict of interest is the credibility attack. Answer it structurally.

If coaches pay you to verify claims about their own businesses, you are the
credit rating agencies in 2007, and a competent journalist will say so.

"We are very careful" is not an answer. This is:

**The athlete is the data subject and the record's owner. The coach licenses
visibility.** The athlete supplies the data, holds the link, consents per story,
and can revoke. The coach can never edit a baseline or a check-in — the product
does not have the code path.

That inverts the incentive: the person whose outcome is being sold is the person
who controls the record. It is already how the software is built, and it should
become the loudest thing in the marketing. Add to it: publish the standard,
publish the failure and revocation rates, and never let a paying customer see a
verification decision before it is made.

## 5. What coaches actually buy — sell that, not virtue

Coaches do not wake up wanting to be trustworthy. They wake up worried about:

1. **Ad accounts.** Meta and Google pull accounts over unsubstantiated earnings
   and health claims. An account ban is an extinction event for an info
   business.
2. **Regulatory exposure.** The FTC's rule on fake and AI-generated testimonials
   (finalised 2024) and the UK's DMCC Act — under which the CMA gained direct
   fining powers of up to 10% of global turnover, without going to court — have
   made unsubstantiated claims genuinely expensive rather than theoretically
   risky.
3. **Conversion.** Verified proof converts better than unverified proof, and
   they can measure that.

So the offer is: **"post claims your payment processor, your ad platform and a
regulator would all accept — and convert better while doing it."** Not "be
honest". *(Confirm the current legal position with a solicitor before any of
this appears in marketing copy.)*

## 6. Sequencing — and a disagreement worth having

Nikita's instinct is ACL first, then the closing mentorships. For the
**product**, that is right: ACL Rehab is warm, live, and gives him his own
story.

For the **mark**, it is backwards. Health is the hardest possible place to
launch a certification body:

- Health claims face the strictest advertising scrutiny there is.
- Anything drifting toward a treatment claim raises the evidentiary bar to
  "competent and reliable scientific evidence" — and can pull the product
  toward medical-device territory, which the drafted standard already warns
  about.
- Rung 3 (third-party system of record) is hard in rehab: there is no Stripe
  for knees. Clinic EMR access is a long procurement, not a signup.

Money verticals are the opposite: Stripe and bank APIs make rung 3 genuinely
achievable, the metric is unambiguous, and the FTC/CMA pressure creates urgency
a salesperson can use.

**Recommended split:**
- **ACL Rehab — first customer of the *product*.** Proves the engine, produces
  the reference, and Nikita's own recovery is the founding story. Verification
  here stays at rungs 0–2 (self-report, artefact, practitioner attestation) and
  is sold as *adherence and engagement evidence*, not clinical outcome proof.
- **Closing / business mentorships — first customers of the *mark*.** Stripe and
  bank data make real verification possible; the buyers are already frightened
  of the FTC. This is where the certification revenue starts.

## 7. Where the money actually is, in the order it arrives

1. **Software membership** *(now)* — console + verified intake. This is the
   $397–797 product that already exists. It funds the rest and it is not where
   the fortune is.
2. **Verification fees** *(next)* — per published claim, or a tier including N
   verified claims a year. Priced like certification, not software: an annual
   licence plus per-audit fees. Certification bodies charge for the audit, not
   the software, and it is a different order of margin.
3. **The registry** *(later, and biggest)* — a public, searchable directory of
   verified coaches, with a hosted proof page per claim. This stops being a
   compliance cost and becomes **lead generation**, which coaches pay far more
   for than software. It is also the thing that makes the mark mean something
   to consumers, because it is the only part the public ever sees.
4. **Licensing the standard** *(speculative)* — a platform or processor wanting
   a way to check advertiser claims. Do not plan on it; it is the upside case.

**A caution on the numbers.** The £250 × 150 coaches by month twelve model
Nikita was shown is not realistic from a cold start; almost nobody acquires 150
paying B2B customers in a first year without existing distribution. What makes
this plausible in his case specifically is that he *is* the distribution — a
closer inside the info space with warm access to NLS, Capital Closer, Forbes
Riley and Six Figure Bookkeepers. Plan for **10–20 paying customers in year
one** and treat anything above that as upside.

## 8. What to build, in order

The standard is drafted. The engine exists. The gap is the evidence pipeline.

1. **`verification_level` (0–4) + an `evidence` table.** Already designed for
   NLS in the T20 schema work; not yet in the ACL fork. Cheap, additive, and
   everything else depends on it.
2. **Evidence upload** — private, encrypted Supabase Storage bucket, athlete
   uploads the artefact, never the coach. Screenshots first, per Nikita's plan.
3. **The review queue** — a private `/admin` route: pending evidence, verify or
   reject, reason recorded. Manual is correct at this stage; it is how you learn
   what good proof looks like before automating it.
4. **The public proof page** — a hosted URL per verified claim, carrying the
   scope disclosure and typicality context from the standard. **The win card
   already built is the visual half of this**; it needs a permanent, checkable
   home behind it.
5. **Revocation** — a claim can be withdrawn and the page updated. Without this
   the mark is worthless the first time something is disputed, and having it is
   itself a marketing asset.
6. **Then APIs** — Stripe first (rung 3 for the money vertical), and only once
   manual review is the bottleneck rather than the product.

## 9. The open questions Nikita should answer before any of it

- Does the mark live under **Milestamp**, or is it a separate brand and entity?
  A certification body that also sells software to the certified has an
  independence problem worth designing around from the start — possibly two
  companies.
- **Who audits?** Nikita alone does not scale past a few dozen coaches, and the
  founder personally verifying claims is itself a credibility question.
- **What is the mark called**, and does it survive a trademark search as a
  certification mark specifically?
- **What happens the first time a paying customer fails verification?** The
  answer to that question, decided in advance and published, is the entire
  credibility of the business.
